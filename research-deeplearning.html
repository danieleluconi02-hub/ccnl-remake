Deep Unsupervised Learning
on Graphic Processors

Building hierarchies of progressively complex distributed representations of sensory data through probabilistic generative models — and discovering that numerosity emerges without supervision.

Try Interactive Demo iDBN on GitHub

Overview

Deep Belief Networks (DBNs) are hierarchical generative models that learn progressively complex distributed representations of sensory data through multiple stacked Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs). Each RBM learns a compressed, higher-level encoding of the layer below, giving the model the capacity to capture structure at multiple scales of abstraction.

At the CCNL, we use DBNs as computational cognitive models — not merely engineering tools. By training DBNs on visual stimuli analogous to those encountered in human perception, we investigate whether cognitive capacities such as numerosity perception, letter recognition, and word-form processing emerge spontaneously from unsupervised statistical learning, without explicit symbolic programming.

This research sits at the intersection of machine learning and cognitive neuroscience, testing the hypothesis that the brain's representational hierarchy arises from the same unsupervised learning pressures that drive deep generative models.

Key Findings

Emergent Numerosity Perception

A DBN trained on images of dot patterns with varying numbers of elements spontaneously develops internal representations that are selective for numerosity — mirroring the numerical tuning curves found in parietal and prefrontal neurons. Crucially, this emerges without any supervision or explicit number information in the training signal.

Stoianov & Zorzi (2012). Nature Neuroscience, 15, 194–196.

Visual Word-Form Representations

DBNs trained on printed letters and words develop features analogous to those found in the Visual Word Form Area of the human brain — from oriented edge detectors in lower layers to whole-word and letter-cluster detectors in higher layers. The representational geometry reflects orthographic similarity between words.

Testolin et al. (2013). Frontiers in Psychology, 4, 566.

⚡

The iDBN Algorithm

Standard layer-wise DBN pre-training freezes lower layers before training higher ones. The iterative DBN (iDBN) instead performs joint updates across all layers simultaneously, yielding richer features and faster convergence. GPU implementations allow training on biologically realistic image datasets at scales previously requiring cluster computing.

Architecture & Training

Each layer of a DBN is a Restricted Boltzmann Machine — a bipartite undirected graphical model with a visible layer v and a hidden layer h, connected by a weight matrix W. The joint energy is:

E(v, h) = −Σi aivi − Σj bjhj − Σij viWijhj

Training uses Contrastive Divergence (CD-k), which approximates the log-likelihood gradient with k steps of Gibbs sampling. A positive phase collects co-activation statistics from the data; a negative phase collects them from the model's fantasy particles. Weight updates follow:

ΔW = μΔWprev + η(⟨vh⟩data − ⟨vh⟩model − λW)

After layer-wise pre-training, the full DBN is optionally fine-tuned by backpropagation using the pre-trained weights as a warm start — a procedure shown by Hinton et al. (2006) to reliably escape the poor local minima that trap randomly initialized deep networks.

GENMOD — ERC-Funded Project

The GENMOD project (European Research Council Advanced Grant, PI: Marco Zorzi) developed large-scale DBN implementations running on clusters of GPUs. The project specifically targeted cognitive phenomena where representational structure may emerge from unsupervised exposure to naturalistic stimuli — bypassing the need for labelled datasets or hand-crafted feature engineering.

A desktop-PC-friendly version of the framework was documented in Testolin et al. (2013) in Frontiers in Psychology, making these tools accessible to cognitive scientists without dedicated HPC infrastructure.
